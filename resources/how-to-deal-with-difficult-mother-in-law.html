How to Deal with a Difficult Mother-in-Law When Your Husband Doesn't Take Your Side

One of the most painful parts of a difficult relationship with a mother-in-law is not always the disagreement itself. It is the feeling that your husband does not understand what you are experiencing, or that he expects you to adjust simply to keep peace in the family.

You may find yourself replaying conversations, anticipating criticism before family visits, or feeling anxious about decisions that involve your in-laws. When you try to explain this to your husband, he may become defensive, minimise the incident or say that you are overreacting. You can then end up feeling alone in a marriage where you hoped to have a teammate.

This situation can be especially complicated in families where parents and adult children share a home, have close financial or caregiving ties, or hold strong expectations about family roles. The aim is not to make your husband choose between you and his mother. It is to help him understand your experience, build a respectful partnership with you and agree on boundaries that work for your family.

An important distinction: Your husband does not have to agree with every interpretation you have of his mother's behaviour to take your feelings seriously. Listening, acknowledging the impact and working with you on a solution are all forms of support.

Why does a difficult mother-in-law situation become a marriage problem?

A disagreement between you and your mother-in-law can gradually become a conflict between you and your husband when the two of you have different expectations about family loyalty, privacy and responsibility. You may want him to acknowledge that a comment was hurtful. He may hear this as criticism of his mother or as a demand to distance himself from his family.

He may be used to the family dynamic

Some behaviours that feel intrusive or hurtful to a person marrying into a family may seem normal to someone who grew up with them. Your husband may have learned to ignore certain comments, avoid confrontation or keep the peace. That does not mean the behaviour is harmless, but it may help explain why he does not immediately recognise its impact on you.

He may feel caught between two important relationships

Your husband may genuinely care about both you and his mother and fear that supporting you will hurt his relationship with her. He may respond by staying silent, asking you to adjust or trying to move past the issue quickly. Although this may reduce tension in the moment, repeated avoidance can leave you feeling unprotected and unheard.

You may be discussing incidents while he sees a pattern of criticism

You may be trying to explain several experiences that have built up over time. He may hear each example as a separate complaint about his mother. It can help to move from listing everything she has done to explaining the pattern, its effect on you and what you need to change.

How to make your husband understand your point of view

The goal of the conversation is not to prove that his mother is a bad person. It is to help him understand what happens to you in these interactions and what you need from him as your partner.

1. Choose a calm moment, not the middle of an argument

Try not to start the conversation immediately after a tense phone call or family visit. When either of you is angry or defensive, it is harder to listen. Choose a private time when you are both able to talk without interruptions.

You could begin with: “I want to talk about something that has been affecting me. I am not asking you to fight with your mother. I want you to understand how these situations feel for me and work out how we can handle them together.”

2. Describe specific behaviour rather than labelling his mother

Statements such as “Your mother is toxic” or “She always interferes” can make your husband feel that he has to defend her character. Describe what happened instead. Be as concrete as possible.

For example: “When my parenting decision was criticised in front of everyone, I felt embarrassed and undermined. I would like us to agree that decisions about our child are discussed between us first.”

Specific examples make it easier to understand what needs to change. Try to separate what you observed from what you think the other person intended. You may not know why a comment was made, but you can still explain its impact on you.

3. Explain the emotional impact, not just the incident

Your husband may focus on whether a particular comment was meant as a joke or whether his mother intended to hurt you. Bring the conversation back to how the repeated experience affects you.

You might say: “Maybe she did not mean to hurt me. But when this happens repeatedly, I start feeling anxious before meeting everyone. I need you to understand that this is affecting my sense of comfort in our family.”

4. Tell him what support would look like

“You never take my side” communicates frustration, but it does not tell him what you need him to do differently. Make the request practical.

  • Listen to you privately before deciding who is right.
  • Acknowledge when a comment or action has hurt you, even if he sees the situation differently.
  • Speak up calmly when a boundary is crossed.
  • Discuss major decisions about your home, finances or children with you first.
  • Avoid sharing private disagreements with relatives without your agreement.

It may help to ask him: “When I tell you that something has hurt me, can you first listen and try to understand before explaining your mother's intention?”

5. Ask him to imagine the situation from your position

Rather than asking him to agree with you immediately, invite him to consider how the situation might feel if the roles were reversed. For example: “How would you feel if my family regularly questioned your decisions or made comments about how you manage our home? What would you want me to do in that moment?”

This is an invitation to empathy, not a way to score points. Give him time to think and listen to his answer too.

6. Recognise his relationship with his mother without dismissing your own needs

You can acknowledge that his mother matters to him and that he may have responsibilities towards his parents. At the same time, being a caring son does not require him to dismiss his wife's distress or agree to every family request.

Try framing the issue as a shared problem: “I respect that your mother is important to you. I am not asking you to stop caring for her. I am asking us to find a way for you to maintain that relationship while we also protect our relationship and our privacy.”

What if your husband says you are overreacting?

Being told that you are too sensitive, making an issue out of nothing or creating unnecessary tension can feel deeply invalidating. Instead of arguing about whether your reaction is too big, explain what you would like him to understand.

You could say: “You may not have experienced it the way I did. I am not asking you to feel exactly what I feel. I am asking you to take seriously that it has affected me.”

If he disagrees about the facts, you can still discuss the impact and the boundary you need going forward. A useful conversation does not require identical memories or interpretations. It does require respect and a willingness to find a workable response.

How to deal with a difficult mother-in-law directly

While your husband has an important role in helping manage his side of the family, you can also decide how you want to respond to difficult interactions. You do not need to answer every comment, defend every decision or enter every disagreement.

Set clear, respectful boundaries

A boundary is not a demand that another person change their personality. It is a clear statement about what you are comfortable with and how you will respond if a situation continues.

  • For personal comments: “I would prefer not to discuss my appearance or personal choices.”
  • For parenting disagreements: “Thank you for your suggestion. We will discuss this and decide what works for our child.”
  • For unplanned visits: “Please let us know before coming over so we can plan our day.”
  • For criticism in front of others: “I am happy to discuss this privately, but I do not want to argue about it in front of everyone.”

Keep the wording calm and consistent. A boundary is more useful when it is realistic and when you know what action you can take if it is not respected.

Do not get pulled into every disagreement

Some conversations are unlikely to lead to understanding. You can choose not to respond to every provocation. A brief answer, a change of subject or stepping away from a heated conversation may be more helpful than trying to win the argument.

This does not mean accepting repeated disrespect. It means choosing where to spend your energy and addressing important issues in a way that has a chance of being constructive.

Discuss household expectations before resentment builds

If you live with your in-laws or see them frequently, discuss practical arrangements with your husband. These might include household responsibilities, personal space, finances, childcare, visitors and how family decisions are made. Vague expectations can create repeated misunderstandings.

What boundaries should a husband help establish with his mother?

When a conflict involves his own parent, a husband may be better placed to communicate a shared decision. This can reduce the risk of the wife being portrayed as the person who is keeping the family apart. The message should be respectful and should reflect an agreement the couple has genuinely reached.

For example, he might say:

  • “We will make decisions about our home together. We will let you know once we have decided.”
  • “Please discuss concerns about our parenting with us privately rather than in front of others.”
  • “We want to spend time with you, but we also need to plan visits around our work and family routine.”
  • “I understand you have a different opinion. We have discussed this and made our decision.”

He does not need to speak harshly or disrespectfully. What matters is consistency: a boundary that is stated once but abandoned whenever there is pressure is unlikely to create lasting change.

What if your husband continues to stay neutral?

Sometimes a husband believes that staying out of the disagreement is the fairest response. But when one person repeatedly experiences criticism or boundary violations, silence can feel like permission for the situation to continue.

Explain that you are not asking him to automatically agree with you or to reject his mother. You are asking him to participate in protecting the relationship you share. Neutrality should not mean leaving you to manage every difficult interaction alone.

You can agree on a simple approach for future incidents:

  1. Either partner can raise a concern privately without being interrupted or dismissed.
  2. You both discuss what happened and what boundary, if any, is needed.
  3. The partner whose family member is involved helps communicate the shared decision where appropriate.
  4. You revisit the agreement if it is not working, rather than repeating the same argument.

When should you consider couple counselling?

Consider professional support when disagreements with in-laws repeatedly turn into arguments between you and your husband, when one partner feels unheard, or when the couple cannot agree on reasonable boundaries. Counselling can provide a structured space to understand each person's perspective, identify recurring patterns and practise conversations that are difficult to have at home.

A culturally informed therapist should understand that family connection, respect for parents and responsibilities towards relatives may be important values. Therapy should not assume that a couple must distance themselves from their families. It can help partners work out how to honour family relationships while making decisions together and protecting their emotional wellbeing.

If your husband is unwilling to attend, you may still choose to seek individual support to explore your feelings, options and boundaries. Individual counselling cannot make another person change, but it can help you decide how you want to respond.

When safety matters more than keeping the peace

There is a difference between a difficult relationship and a situation involving intimidation, threats, coercion, financial control, isolation or physical violence. If you feel unsafe, prioritise individual support and safety planning. Joint counselling or direct confrontation may not be appropriate where there is a risk of retaliation. Seek help from trusted people and relevant local support services; contact emergency services if you are in immediate danger.

A healthier way forward: build a partnership, not a battle

It is possible to respect your husband's relationship with his mother while asking for respect, privacy and emotional security in your marriage. It is also possible for your husband to love his mother and still recognise that some behaviours affect his wife negatively.

The key question is not “Whose side are you on?” It is “How do we handle this as partners?” When both people can listen without becoming defensive, identify the behaviour that needs to change and agree on consistent boundaries, the couple has a better chance of addressing the conflict without turning it into a permanent family divide.

Questions you can discuss with your husband

  • What does support from each other look like when one of our families causes hurt?
  • Which decisions should remain between the two of us?
  • What boundaries would help us feel comfortable at home and during family visits?
  • How can we raise concerns without either person feeling that they must defend their entire family?
  • What will we do if a boundary is repeatedly ignored?
