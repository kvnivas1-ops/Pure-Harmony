How to Handle In-Law Conflicts in Marriage

Marriage can bring two people into a relationship while also connecting two families. For some couples, this brings practical help, belonging and a strong support network. For others, differences in expectations, household roles, privacy, finances or parenting can become a recurring source of tension.

In-law conflict is not limited to one gender, one generation or one type of household. It can arise in joint families, nuclear families and relationships where relatives live in another city but remain closely involved in decisions. The goal is not to label family members as the problem. It is to understand what is happening and agree on boundaries that protect the couple’s wellbeing while allowing respectful family relationships where possible.

Why can in-law disagreements affect a marriage?

Different expectations about family roles

Partners may have grown up with different ideas about how often to visit parents, how decisions should be made, how much care adult children should provide and what privacy a married couple should expect. Neither partner may have discussed these assumptions before marriage.

Feeling caught between a partner and parents

A person may feel responsible for keeping peace with their parents while also wanting their spouse to feel supported. If the couple has not agreed how to handle disagreements, one partner may experience the other’s silence as a lack of support, while the other experiences the situation as an impossible choice.

Household decisions and privacy

Disagreements may involve finances, household responsibilities, childcare, living arrangements, visits or decisions about work. When relatives have a strong role in these areas, partners may struggle to find space for private discussion and shared decision-making.

Research on Indian marital relationships highlights that family structure, kinship expectations and household decision-making can shape relationship experiences. A recent narrative review also notes that conflicts about relatives, money, chores and children appear in the literature on Indian couples. These findings describe broad social patterns; they should not be used to assume what is happening in any one family.

How couples can manage in-law conflict

1. Discuss the issue privately as partners first

Before responding to a parent or relative, make time to understand each other’s perspective. What happened? What felt uncomfortable? What does each partner need now? Try to avoid turning the conversation into a test of loyalty.

2. Agree on the boundary together

A boundary is a clear agreement about what the couple can accept and what they will do if a situation becomes difficult. Examples may include discussing major financial decisions together, agreeing on visits in advance or keeping disagreements between partners private rather than involving several relatives.

Boundaries should be realistic and proportionate. They do not have to mean cutting off family contact. They should also account for genuine caregiving responsibilities, shared housing, financial dependence and cultural or religious commitments.

3. Communicate with your own family where possible

When a concern involves one partner’s parents, it may be more constructive for that partner to communicate the couple’s shared decision. This can reduce the risk of presenting the spouse as the person who is “stopping” family contact. The message should remain respectful and clear.

4. Use specific language instead of character judgments

“Your family interferes in everything” is likely to create defensiveness. A more useful description might be: “When our financial decisions are discussed before we have agreed on them, I feel that we do not have enough privacy. Can we decide together what we share?”

5. Make room for differences without surrendering important needs

Partners do not need identical relationships with each other’s families. One may prefer frequent visits; the other may need more quiet time. The couple can look for arrangements that respect both preferences while protecting safety, dignity and autonomy.

What if the conflict is affecting the relationship?

Pay attention if conversations about relatives repeatedly turn into arguments between partners, if one person feels unable to express a concern, or if decisions are made under pressure. It may help to identify the couple’s shared priorities and agree on a consistent way to handle future situations.

In some families, the difficulty is not simply a communication problem. Threats, humiliation, isolation, financial control, restrictions on movement or violence require a more careful response. If someone feels unsafe, individual support and safety planning should take priority over joint conversations or couple therapy.

Can couple therapy help with in-law conflict?

Couple therapy can help partners understand how family expectations are affecting their relationship, communicate about difficult decisions and develop boundaries they can sustain. A therapist should take the couple’s cultural and family context seriously rather than assuming that independence or closeness with relatives has the same meaning for everyone.

Therapy is not about deciding that one family is right and the other is wrong. It can help partners clarify what they can negotiate, what they need to protect and how they want to approach family relationships as a team.

Questions to discuss together

  • Which family expectations are important to each of us?
  • Where do we need more privacy or shared decision-making?
  • What responsibilities do we have towards parents or relatives?
  • How can we communicate a boundary respectfully and consistently?
  • What will we do if we disagree about a family request?
