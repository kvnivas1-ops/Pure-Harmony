What Is Couple Therapy and How Does It Work?

Couple therapy is a form of psychological support in which partners work with a trained professional to understand difficulties in their relationship, communicate about them and consider what needs to change. It is also called couples counselling or marriage counselling.

It is not a courtroom where a therapist decides who is right. Nor is it a promise that every relationship will continue. The work is to understand each partner’s experience, identify patterns that keep causing distress and help the couple make thoughtful decisions about the relationship.

In brief: Couples do not need to be on the verge of separation to consider therapy. It may be useful when the same arguments keep returning, trust has been affected, communication has become difficult or partners want help navigating a major life transition.

What happens in couple therapy?

The exact process varies with the therapist, the couple’s circumstances and the concerns they bring. In general, sessions create a structured setting where each partner can explain what has been happening and what they hope will be different.

Understanding each partner’s perspective

Partners may describe the same event in very different ways. A therapist helps slow the conversation down so that both experiences can be heard. This does not mean every behaviour is acceptable or that both people are always equally responsible for a problem.

Identifying repeated relationship patterns

Some couples find themselves caught in a familiar cycle: one person raises a concern, the other feels criticised and withdraws, and the first person becomes more insistent. The original topic may be money, time, parenting or family boundaries, but the interaction itself becomes part of the difficulty.

Therapy can help partners notice the sequence and explore the feelings, expectations and needs underneath it. The goal is not to assign a fixed label to either partner, but to understand what happens between them.

Setting goals and reviewing progress

Goals may include having difficult conversations more safely, rebuilding trust, navigating parenting disagreements, making decisions about the future or understanding whether the relationship can continue. Goals should be realistic and revisited as circumstances change.

What concerns can bring couples to therapy?

  • Recurring arguments that never seem to reach a resolution.
  • Communication that has become critical, defensive or avoidant.
  • Emotional distance or feeling lonely within the relationship.
  • Trust concerns following betrayal, secrecy or broken agreements.
  • Disagreements about parenting, finances, intimacy or responsibilities.
  • Pressure involving parents, in-laws or extended family.
  • Changes after marriage, relocation, parenthood, illness or career transitions.
  • Uncertainty about whether to repair the relationship or separate.

These experiences do not automatically mean a relationship is unhealthy or that therapy is the only answer. They are examples of concerns couples may choose to explore with a professional.

Does couple therapy mean the marriage is failing?

No. Seeking support does not mean that a couple has failed. Some partners come because they want to address a recurring problem before resentment grows. Others seek help after a major rupture or when they are uncertain about the future.

Therapy should not pressure people to stay together at any cost. Depending on the circumstances, the work may involve rebuilding the relationship, improving how partners navigate a difficult period, or reaching a clearer understanding of whether continuing together is right for them.

What is the difference between couple therapy and individual therapy?

Couple therapy focuses on the relationship and the interaction between partners. Individual therapy focuses on one person’s experiences, needs and goals. A person may choose to begin individual support when their partner is not ready to attend, but this is not the same as working together as a couple and cannot make decisions on an absent partner’s behalf.

Is couple therapy suitable for every relationship?

Not always. When there is violence, coercive control, intimidation or fear of a partner, joint sessions may be unsafe or inappropriate. Individual safety assessment and specialist support should take priority. A therapist should consider these circumstances before recommending joint work.

What does research say about couple therapy?

Research reviews have found that some structured couple interventions can improve relationship distress and related outcomes for many participants. However, studies differ in their methods, participants, therapeutic approaches and follow-up periods. Findings from a research trial cannot predict what will happen for a particular couple, and therapy does not guarantee reconciliation.

For an overview of the evidence, see the references below. These studies discuss couple interventions generally; they should not be read as a claim that every therapist uses the same model.

How can you decide whether to take the first step?

You might start by asking: What feels hardest between us at the moment? What have we already tried? What would each of us hope to understand or change? You do not need to agree on every detail before speaking to a therapist.
