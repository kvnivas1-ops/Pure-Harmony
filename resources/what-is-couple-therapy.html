“We don’t fight anymore. We just don’t talk.”

I hear versions of this sentence quite often in my sessions with couples.

Sometimes the couple sitting in front of me has been married for 15 years. Sometimes they have been together for only a year. Sometimes they are in an arranged marriage and are still figuring out how to become partners. Sometimes they have been dating for years but are struggling after getting married.

And sometimes, they tell me, “We are not sure if our problems are serious enough for therapy.”

My answer is usually simple: you don’t have to wait until your marriage is falling apart to ask for help.

Marital relationship counseling is not about deciding who is right and who is wrong. It is about understanding what is happening between two people who may still love each other but have lost the ability to reach each other.

As a couple therapist, this is the part of the work I find most meaningful. I help couples slow down, understand the patterns they have fallen into, and learn how to respond to each other differently.

Because very often, the problem is not the husband or the wife.

The problem is the cycle they have created together.

What Is Marital Relationship Counseling?

Marital relationship counseling, also called couples therapy, is a form of therapy where partners work with a trained therapist to understand and address difficulties in their relationship.

That can mean many things.

Maybe you argue about money every month.

Maybe one of you feels that you are carrying the entire household while the other feels constantly criticised.

Maybe your husband comes home from work and wants silence, while you want to talk about everything that happened during the day.

Maybe your wife wants more emotional connection, while you feel that nothing you do is ever enough.

Maybe your in-laws have become a constant source of conflict.

Maybe you have stopped being physically intimate.

Maybe there has been an affair.

Or maybe nothing dramatic has happened at all. You simply feel like you have become roommates instead of partners.

These problems can look very different on the surface. But when I work with couples, I often find that there is something underneath them that sounds more like:

“I don’t feel important to you anymore.”

“I don’t feel understood by you.”

“I am scared that we are drifting apart.”

“I don’t know how to reach you anymore.”

That is where relationship counseling can help.

It creates a space where both people can finally say what they have been trying to say at home, without the conversation immediately becoming another argument.

Why Do Indian Couples Struggle in Their Marriages?

Marriage in India does not involve just two people.

It often involves two families, different expectations, cultural beliefs, financial responsibilities, gender roles, traditions and, sometimes, completely different ideas of what marriage is supposed to look like.

This can make relationships complicated in ways that are not always easy to explain.

Consider a couple who has recently married.

The husband believes, “My parents have always been there for me. Of course they will be involved in our lives.”

The wife feels, “I married you, not your entire family. Why does every decision have to involve your parents?”

Neither necessarily intends to hurt the other.

But very quickly, the conversation becomes:

“You always take your mother’s side.”

“You don’t respect my parents.”

And suddenly, they are fighting about the mother-in-law when the deeper issue is actually:

“Do I have a secure place in this marriage?”

I see similar patterns around household responsibilities.

A husband may genuinely believe he is contributing because he pays the bills and takes care of major financial decisions.

His wife may be thinking:

Who remembers what groceries we need? Who manages the children’s schoolwork? Who plans family functions? Who notices when the house needs something?

Both may feel they are doing their part.

Both may feel unappreciated.

And both may be right from their own perspective.

This is why simply telling couples to “communicate better” is rarely enough.

We need to understand what is actually happening underneath the communication.

When Should You Consider Couple Therapy?

You do not need to be on the verge of divorce to seek therapy.

In fact, I would encourage couples to come much earlier.

Here are some of the signs I would pay attention to.

You keep having the same argument

The topic changes, but the fight remains the same.

Today it is about money. Tomorrow it is about your mother. Next week it is about the children.

But underneath every argument is the same feeling of not being heard or valued.

You have stopped talking

Not fighting can sometimes look like a healthy relationship. It isn’t always.

If both of you have stopped bringing things up because you already know how the conversation will end, there may be emotional distance developing.

You eat dinner together. You discuss the children. You talk about bills.

But you no longer talk about each other.

You feel lonely despite being married

This is one of the most painful experiences I see.

You have a partner. You share a home. You may even sleep in the same room.

But emotionally, you feel alone.

Your relationship has changed after having children

Many couples are surprised by how much parenthood changes their marriage.

The relationship that once revolved around two people now revolves around school, homework, meals, grandparents, activities and responsibilities.

One partner may feel neglected. The other may feel overwhelmed. Neither knows how to reconnect.

Your families are becoming a constant source of conflict

Questions around in-laws, boundaries, festivals, visits, financial support and family expectations can become extremely sensitive.

The issue is often not whether your parents or your partner are “good” or “bad”.

It is about learning how to create boundaries while still respecting family relationships.

There has been a breach of trust

Infidelity is one of the most obvious reasons couples seek therapy.

But trust can be damaged in many other ways too.

Repeated lying, hidden finances, secret conversations, emotional involvement with someone else or breaking important promises can gradually make a partner feel unsafe in the relationship.

Intimacy has disappeared

Couples often find it easier to discuss money, children and in-laws than sex.

But sexual and emotional intimacy are important parts of a relationship.

A mismatch in desire, unresolved resentment, childbirth, stress, body-image concerns or simply years of emotional distance can affect physical intimacy.

These conversations can feel uncomfortable.

Therapy gives couples a place to have them without shame or judgment.

What Actually Happens in Couple Therapy?

One of the first things I tell couples is that I am not there to decide who is right.

You may come into the room convinced that your partner is the problem.

You may have a list.

“He doesn’t listen.”

“She is always angry.”

“He never helps.”

“She gives more importance to her family.”

“He doesn’t care about me.”

My job is not to choose one side.

My job is to understand the pattern.

For example, imagine a wife repeatedly asking her husband to spend more time with her.

He feels criticised and starts withdrawing.

The more he withdraws, the more anxious she becomes.

So she asks more questions, complains more and pushes harder.

He withdraws even further.

Now she thinks, “See? He doesn’t care.”

And he thinks, “See? Nothing I do is enough.”

This is the cycle.

If we only focus on the individual behaviours, we might say she needs to stop complaining or he needs to communicate more.

But the real work is understanding why this cycle keeps repeating.

Once couples can see the cycle, they can begin to work against it instead of against each other.

What Do We Work On in Marriage Counseling?

Learning to communicate without attacking

There is a huge difference between:

“You never care about me.”

and

“I feel alone when I have to handle everything by myself.”

The first makes the other person defensive. The second gives them something they can understand.

I often help couples move away from accusations and towards expressing the feeling and need underneath them.

This does not mean suppressing anger.

It means learning how to communicate anger without turning your partner into the enemy.

Learning to listen

Listening is not waiting for your turn to respond.

Sometimes your partner is telling you something painful and your immediate reaction is:

“But that’s not what happened.”

“You are misunderstanding me.”

“You also do the same thing.”

You may be right.

But if your partner does not feel heard, proving your point will not create connection.

Couples therapy helps partners learn to pause, understand what the other person is experiencing and respond without immediately becoming defensive.

Understanding your own triggers

Sometimes the intensity of our reaction is bigger than the situation in front of us.

A partner coming home late may trigger fear of being unimportant.

A husband becoming quiet may trigger a wife’s fear of abandonment.

A wife questioning spending may trigger a husband’s feeling that he is being controlled.

These reactions often have a history.

Understanding that history does not mean blaming your childhood or your parents for everything.

It means understanding why certain situations affect you so deeply and learning to respond differently.

Setting boundaries with families

This is particularly important for Indian couples.

Your parents can be important to you without becoming a third person in your marriage.

Your partner can have a different relationship with your parents without being disrespectful.

Couples often need help learning how to say:

“This is something we need to decide together.”

without turning it into:

“Your family versus my family.”

Healthy boundaries do not mean cutting people off.

They mean knowing where the marriage begins and where the extended family ends.

Rebuilding trust

When trust has been broken, couples often want to know:

“How long will it take before I can trust you again?”

There is no fixed timeline.

Trust is rebuilt through repeated experiences of honesty, transparency, accountability and emotional safety.

The hurt partner needs space to express the pain.

The partner who caused the hurt needs to understand that saying “I already apologised” does not automatically make the wound disappear.

Therapy can help both partners move through that process without getting trapped in endless blame and defensiveness.

What About Arranged Marriages?

This is an area I believe deserves much more attention.

In many Indian families, marriage begins with compatibility questions that are practical:

What does he do? What does she do? Where will you live? What are the family backgrounds? What are the financial expectations? What are your qualifications?

But compatibility is much bigger than these questions.

Can you disagree safely? Can you talk about money? How do you handle anger? What role will parents have in your marriage? How will household responsibilities be divided? What are your expectations around children? What does emotional intimacy mean to each of you? What are your expectations around physical intimacy? What happens when one person wants to live with their parents and the other doesn’t?

These conversations are uncomfortable.

They are also important.

I have seen couples struggle not because they are fundamentally incompatible, but because they entered marriage without ever having these conversations.

Couples therapy can be useful not only when an arranged marriage is struggling, but also before marriage, when a couple wants to understand each other more deeply.

Does Couples Therapy Mean Your Marriage Is Failing?

I don’t see it that way.

Coming to therapy does not mean you have failed at marriage.

It means you have recognised that something is not working and you are willing to understand it.

In fact, I often wish couples came earlier.

By the time many couples enter therapy, they have spent years repeating the same patterns.

They have accumulated resentment. They have stopped expecting change. They may have already emotionally disconnected.

It is much harder to repair a relationship after years of emotional distance than it is to address a problem when it first appears.

You don’t need to wait until someone has had an affair.

You don’t need to wait until someone moves out.

You don’t need to wait until the word “divorce” becomes part of every argument.

You can come when you simply realise:

“We don’t know how to talk to each other anymore.”

That is enough.

Can Marriage Counseling Actually Save a Marriage?

I would be careful with the word “save”.

Therapy cannot promise that every marriage will continue.

And I don’t believe the purpose of therapy is to keep every couple together at any cost.

The purpose is to help both people understand the relationship clearly and make healthier decisions.

For many couples, that means rebuilding a marriage they had almost given up on.

For others, it means learning how to stay connected while working through a difficult period.

And sometimes, therapy helps a couple recognise that the relationship cannot continue in its current form and navigate that reality with greater clarity and respect.

What therapy can do is give you a better chance of understanding each other before making decisions that cannot easily be undone.

My Approach to Couple Therapy

When couples come to me, I don’t expect them to arrive knowing how to communicate perfectly.

Most of us were never taught how.

We learn how to study. We learn how to work. We learn how to manage money.

But very few of us are taught how to handle conflict, express vulnerability, repair after hurting someone or build emotional intimacy.

So I don’t see couples as two people who need to be fixed.

I see two people who have developed ways of protecting themselves.

Sometimes those protective behaviours are now hurting the relationship.

My role is to help you understand them, slow down the cycle and create a different way of relating to each other.

And that work can begin even if you are not sure whether your relationship is “bad enough” for therapy.

You Don’t Have to Wait Until Your Marriage Is in Crisis

If you are constantly fighting, feeling emotionally disconnected, struggling with intimacy, dealing with family boundaries, recovering from a betrayal or simply feeling that something between you has changed, it may be worth talking to someone.

You may come into therapy saying:

“We don’t know if this can be fixed.”

That is okay.

You don’t need to know the answer before you come.

We can start with understanding what is happening.

Because sometimes the couple who sits across from me saying, “We have tried everything”, doesn’t actually need to try harder.

They need to understand each other differently.

If you and your partner are going through something similar, I offer couple therapy to help you understand the patterns in your relationship, communicate more openly and work towards a healthier connection.

You don’t have to figure it out alone.
